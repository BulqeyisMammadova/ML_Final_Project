\section{Introduction}
Real-estate asking prices vary with area, location, floor and property
type. A scraped listing dataset adds duplicate advertisements, missing
values and extreme prices, so the modelling problem includes careful
data preparation as well as algorithm implementation. We frame the
same listings as two tasks: predict sale price and classify whether a
listing is above the training-set median price.

Our team built decision trees and Pegasos SVMs from scratch in NumPy,
then compared them with scikit-learn models under a shared split and
matched settings where possible. We report validation curves, held-out
metrics, training-only cross-validation, runtime and error analysis.
The objective is a reproducible comparison that explains model behaviour,
including where high-price listings challenge regression.

\section{Related Work / Background}
Decision trees choose feature thresholds that reduce node impurity and
recursively partition the feature space. Gini and entropy serve the
classification task; variance reduction serves price regression.
Their interpretable splits can model nonlinear interactions but can
overfit without depth and leaf-size controls. A soft-margin SVM instead
optimises regularised hinge loss. Pegasos uses stochastic sub-gradients
with a decreasing learning rate~\cite{pegasos}. Random Fourier features
provide a finite-dimensional approximation to an RBF kernel and allow
the same linear solver to represent nonlinear boundaries.

\section{Data and Preprocessing}
The Kaggle bina.az sale dataset~\cite{binaaz} has 100,775 rows before
cleaning. We keep the latest scrape of each listing URL, leaving 64,454
unique listings. The price currency in the downloaded version is AZN.
Area strings are parsed into square metres, including conversion from
\emph{sot}; floor and total-floor counts are extracted from combined
strings. The model uses area, floor, total floors, coordinates, room
count, land area and one-hot property type (14 encoded features).
We remove \texttt{unit\_price} and \texttt{total\_price} because they
are calculated from the target, and we exclude listing identifiers,
names and raw addresses. Missing numeric values use training medians;
unavailable land area uses zero because it is inapplicable to many flats.

The target is positive sale price; regression fits $\log(1+\text{price})$
and returns predictions to AZN for evaluation. The binary label is
premium when price exceeds the training median of 210,000 AZN.
The split sizes are shown in Table~\ref{tab:data}; the validation and
test halves are stratified by this fixed label. Exploratory analysis
found a heavy right tail and some suspicious low prices or possible
rental entries. An exploratory 99th-percentile training filter reduced
typical MAE but increased RMSE on unchanged validation data, so the
integrated pipeline retains high-price observations rather than applying
an unsupported universal outlier cutoff.

Figure~\ref{fig:eda} shows the training-price distribution. Raw prices
are strongly right-skewed (the mean is 1.57 times the median), while
$\log(1+\text{price})$ is close to symmetric, which motivates fitting the
regression models on the log scale. The median split gives an almost
balanced tier label (49.5\,\% premium in training).

\section{Method}
\subsection{Decision tree (from scratch)}
For each numeric feature, the tree sorts training values, considers
midpoints between distinct adjacent values, and selects the threshold
with the greatest weighted impurity decrease. Classification supports
Gini impurity and entropy; regression uses mean squared deviation within
nodes. Recursion stops according to maximum depth, minimum split and
leaf sample counts, or minimum impurity decrease. A regression leaf
predicts its mean target, while a classification leaf predicts its
majority class and exposes class-frequency probabilities. All split
search, recursion and prediction are implemented in NumPy. Tree depth,
leaf size and classification criterion are selected on validation data.

To keep the split search fast, each feature is sorted once and the
left/right class counts (or $\sum y$ and $\sum y^2$) for every candidate
threshold are obtained with cumulative sums; this returns the same split
as the naive search and was about $180\times$ faster on 3,000 rows.
A unit test builds our tree and scikit-learn's on small tie-free
examples (Gini, entropy and MSE) and checks that every split feature,
threshold and leaf matches. On the real data we found that the midpoint
$(a+b)/2$ of two almost equal coordinates can round to $b$ in floating
point, sending every sample to one child and causing infinite recursion;
as in scikit-learn we fall back to $a$ in that case and refuse empty
splits, and a regression test guards it.

\subsection{Random forest from our own trees (bonus)}
For Problem~9 we averaged 50 of our own trees. Each tree is grown on a
bootstrap sample of the training rows and considers only two randomly
chosen features at each split (\texttt{max\_features}$=2$,
\texttt{min\_samples\_leaf}$=5$); features that are constant in a node
are skipped, as in scikit-learn. Regression averages the tree
predictions; classification averages the leaf class frequencies.

\subsection{Support-vector machine (from scratch)}
We map the standard and premium labels to $-1$ and $+1$, respectively,
and minimize the regularized hinge-loss objective:
\begin{equation}
  J(\mathbf{w},b)=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^{2}
  +\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\!\big(0,\,1-y_i(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i+b)\big).
\end{equation}
For the linear model, the margin width is $2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_2$.
We identify training points on or inside the margin as support vectors:
\begin{equation}
  y_i(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i+b)\leq 1.
\end{equation}
At update $t$, Pegasos samples training example $i_t$ and uses
$\eta_t=1/(\lambda t)$. Its stochastic updates are
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{w}_t &= (1-\eta_t\lambda)\mathbf{w}_{t-1}
    +\eta_t y_{i_t}\mathbf{x}_{i_t}\,
      \mathbb{1}\!\left[y_{i_t}(\mathbf{w}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i_t}
      +b_{t-1})<1\right],\\
  b_t &= b_{t-1}+\eta_t y_{i_t}\,
      \mathbb{1}\!\left[y_{i_t}(\mathbf{w}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i_t}
      +b_{t-1})<1\right].
\end{align}
The bias is unregularized: it is updated on margin violations but is not
multiplied by the weight-decay factor. For the nonlinear model, random
Fourier features approximate an RBF kernel:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x}) &= [z_j(\mathbf{x})]_{j=1}^{D},\\
    z_j(\mathbf{x}) &= \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{D}}\cos(\phi_j(\mathbf{x})),\\
    \phi_j(\mathbf{x}) &= \boldsymbol{\omega}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x}+c_j,\\
    \boldsymbol{\omega}_j &\sim \mathcal{N}(0,2\gamma\mathbf{I}),\\
    c_j &\sim \mathcal{U}(0,2\pi), \quad j=1,\ldots,D.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Pegasos is applied to $\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})$; its transformed-space
coefficients are not interpreted as original-feature importance.
We chose random Fourier features rather than kernelised Pegasos because
a kernel model must store a coefficient per training example and
evaluate kernels against the whole training set at prediction time,
which is costly for 45,118 rows. With RFF the cost is fixed by $D$
(256 components), the same linear Pegasos code is reused unchanged, and
the bandwidth $\gamma$ remains a single tunable hyperparameter.

\section{Experimental Setup}
All experiments used seed 42. After deduplication, 70\% of the rows were
reserved for training. The premium threshold was set to the training
price median; the remaining rows were split into validation and test
halves stratified by that label. Missing numeric features were filled
with training medians, except inapplicable land area, which used zero.
Property types were one-hot encoded using training categories only.
The team's SVM used training means and standard
deviations; scikit-learn's hinge-loss baseline scaled features inside
a pipeline. Every cross-validation fold refitted its own preprocessing
on that fold's training rows.

Tree depth, minimum leaf size, Gini versus entropy, SVM regularisation,
and RFF bandwidth were chosen from validation results only. The regression
selection metric was log-RMSE; classification used premium-class F1.
The test rows were evaluated once with the selected settings. We report
price-scale RMSE, MAE, and $R^2$, plus precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC,
and confusion matrices for price tiers. NumPy implementations computed
the metrics. We compared the trees with matched scikit-learn trees and
the linear Pegasos SVM with a scaled SGD hinge-loss classifier. Wall-clock
fit and prediction times were measured separately and recorded by the
reproduction script; they depend on the machine.

The headline numbers come from \texttt{python -m src.run\_all}. The
additional overfitting curve, EDA figure and SVM schedule study use the
same split and training/validation rows only
(\texttt{python -m src.report\_extras}). The decision-tree experiments
and the random forest bonus (\texttt{python -m src.exp\_tree}) and the
geospatial bonus (\texttt{python geospatial\_bonus.py}) were developed
earlier on the shared \texttt{get\_splits()} split with 9,668 validation
and 9,668 test rows; their numbers are therefore reported separately and
compared only with models trained on the same rows.

\section{Results}
The reproducible run retained 64,454 unique listings. The seeded split
contained 45,118 training, 9,667 validation, and 9,669 test rows. The
premium threshold was the training median of 210,000 AZN. The 14 encoded
features included area, floors, coordinates, room count, land area, and
one-hot property type. The category vocabulary came from training rows only.
All configurations below were selected using validation results before
the test set was evaluated. Three-fold cross-validation (CV) uses training
rows only and is reported separately from the single held-out test score.

The linear Pegasos model learned $\|w\|=3.464$ and a margin width of
$2/\|w\|=0.577$ in standardised feature space. By the criterion
$y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\leq1$, 23,410 of 45,118 training rows were support
vectors. Its regularised hinge objective fell from 15.091 after 100
updates to 0.580 after 20,000 updates, with stochastic fluctuations
(Fig.~\ref{fig:svm-objective}).
The RFF model improved the held-out F1 over linear Pegasos from 0.777
to 0.806; its three-fold CV F1 was $0.807\pm0.006$. On validation,
$\gamma=1$ beat $\gamma=0.1$ (F1 0.796 vs.\ 0.785 at $\lambda=10^{-3}$).

Table~\ref{tab:svm-lambda} shows how regularisation trades margin for
fit. A larger $\lambda$ shrinks $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ and widens the
margin (0.86 at $\lambda=10^{-2}$); a very small $\lambda$ gives a narrow

The tree classifier had 470 false positives and 441 false negatives on
the held-out set. Area contributed 54.3\% of its total impurity decrease,
followed by longitude (19.2\%) and latitude (9.6\%). For the regression
tree, area contributed 59.0\%, latitude and longitude 11.8\% and 11.6\%,
and the land property type and land area about 7\% each. These are
predictive associations, not causal effects.

Because the linear SVM is trained on standardised features, its weights
are comparable in size. The largest positive weights are room count
(2.28), the office type (1.25) and area (1.09); the garage type has the
most negative weight ($-1.65$). Room count and area are strongly
correlated, so the SVM splits their shared effect differently from the
tree, which puts most importance on area. A linear boundary cannot
represent the location effect as well as the tree, which partly explains
the gap between linear Pegasos (F1 0.777) and the tree (F1 0.904).

Table~\ref{tab:time} lists fit and prediction times. scikit-learn's
compiled trees fit 3--5$\times$ faster than our NumPy trees; our linear
Pegasos fit is as fast as \texttt{SGDClassifier}. The RFF model is
slower to predict because every input is mapped to 256 cosine features.

Price errors were much larger in the luxury tail. The 85 test listings
above the training 99th-percentile price of 2.5 million AZN had mean
absolute error 3.64 million AZN, versus 72.0 thousand AZN for the
remaining 9,584 test listings. The median absolute error across the
whole test set was 24.7 thousand AZN. Our tree underpredicted 90.6\%
of those 85 tail listings, indicating systematic compression of high
prices. The reproducible run also saves a log-scale actual-versus-predicted
plot for detailed inspection.

\emph{Random forest (bonus).} This experiment was run with the tree
experiment script on its own seeded split (Task~A on $\log$ price), so
we compare it only with a single tree on the same rows. On the held-out
rows the forest raised $R^2$ from 0.781 to 0.832 (log-MAE 0.202 to
0.170) on Task~A and F1 from 0.908 to 0.934 (ROC-AUC 0.938 to 0.984) on
Task~B; scikit-learn's \texttt{RandomForestRegressor} with the same
settings reached $R^2=0.810$. Validation $R^2$ grows quickly with the
number of trees and flattens after about 30 (Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Validation curves show that allowing deeper trees improves both tasks
over the shallowest settings, but without a leaf-size limit validation
error rises again after depth 10 while training error keeps falling;
minimum leaf size constrains this variance, and averaging bootstrapped
trees in the forest reduces it further. Matching scikit-learn trees give nearly identical test scores,
while their training is faster in this environment. Our SVM's learned
margin and performance depend on feature scaling, regularisation and
the stochastic update budget. RFF improves premium-class F1, but its
transformed coefficients cannot be read as original-feature effects.

The price distribution has an extreme upper tail and possible mislabeled
sale/rental listings. The model compresses expensive predictions, so its
overall RMSE is much larger than its median error. Property-type and
coordinate features only approximate neighbourhood and condition;
unavailable text, sparse categories and imperfect listing labels limit
generalisation. Cross-validation standard deviations describe variation
across three training folds, not a confidence interval for the held-out
test result. Timings are machine-specific and the SVM and scikit-learn
optimisers do not have identical update rules.

\section{Conclusion}
The from-scratch trees closely matched scikit-learn on both sale-price
and price-tier tasks. The RFF Pegasos model improved tier F1 over its
linear version, while luxury properties remained the main regression
failure. As bonuses, a random forest built from our own trees improved
both tasks over a single tree, and k-means clusters of the coordinates
showed clear location-based price segments. Future work should verify suspicious listings, improve location
and property-condition features, and evaluate models on a later scrape
to measure performance under market change.
